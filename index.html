AP Environmental Science Topic 9.10 Curriculum Guide

1. What is Habitat Fragmentation?

Habitat fragmentation occurs when large, continuous tracts of forest or ecosystems are carved into smaller, isolated patches by roads, agriculture, logging, or urbanization. It reduces total habitat area and increases the ratio of edge to interior.

2. Abiotic & Biotic Edge Effects (The 1-Hectare Rule)

The microclimate of a forest edge differs dramatically from deep interior forest:

  • Abiotic: Higher ambient temperatures, lower soil humidity, greater solar radiation, and increased wind shear (tree blowdowns).
  • Biotic: Proliferation of opportunistic edge predators (raccoons, foxes, crows), brood parasitism by Brown-headed Cowbirds, and easy colonization by invasive exotic weeds.

3. Core Specialists vs. Edge Generalists

Core Specialists (e.g., Northern Spotted Owl) depend on stable, undisturbed old-growth canopies with low temperature fluctuations and abundant canopy prey. They exhibit a minimum area requirement (modeled here as 2×2 ha contiguous core).
Edge Generalists (e.g., White-tailed Deer) thrive on edge browse, shrub thickets, and transitional ecotones.

4. Wildlife Corridors as Anthropogenic Mitigation

Wildlife overpasses, underpasses, and vegetative corridors bridge fragmented patches across transportation barriers. While a 1-hectare-wide corridor is ecologically composed of edge habitat, it critically restores metapopulation gene flow, reduces roadkill mortality, enables seasonal migration, and prevents inbreeding depression.

5. MacArthur-Wilson Island Biogeography & The SLOSS Debate

Fragmented terrestrial patches behave like ecological islands. Under MacArthur and Wilson's equilibrium model, smaller, isolated patches experience higher extinction rates and lower colonization rates. In reserve design, Single Large (SL) is almost always superior to Several Small (SS) for protecting apex predators and sensitive core specialists.
